# Article 121 fact notes

Companion to `timeline.json` and `airframes.json`. Every number below was checked against the cited source during this research pass. A few blocked sites (af.mil, nationalmuseum.af.mil, fai.org, iwm.org.uk) were seen only through search-result text or verbatim copies, and are flagged where that matters. Where reputable sources disagree, all values are given and the disagreement is stated. Use the wording in the "safe phrasing" lines for hero copy.

## 1. What "Article 121" is, and when it first flew

- **Article 121 is the first Lockheed A-12, USAF serial 60-6924.** "Article" was the CIA/Lockheed production number; A-12s ran from Article 121 (60-6924) to Article 133 (60-6939), and the two M-21s were Articles 134 and 135 (60-6940 and 60-6941). Source: CIA, Robarge, *Archangel: CIA's Supersonic A-12 Reconnaissance Aircraft*, 2nd ed., "Inventory of A-12s", p. 50 (https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/b45f5f8f5e4937963d9e9931313d84a4/Archangel-CIAs-Supersonic-A-12-Reconnaissance-Aircraft.pdf).
- **Built and moved.** Built and ground tested in Burbank in January and February 1962. It left the Skunk Works by road in a crate 35 ft wide and 105 ft long on the night of 26 February 1962 for a two-day trip to the Nevada test site (Groom Lake), with the wings shipped separately. Source: Archangel, p. 16.
- **First flight: three dates appear in print.**
  - **25 April 1962**: unofficial, unannounced hop by Lockheed test pilot Lou Schalk. He flew under two miles at about 20 ft and landed on the lakebed beyond the runway because of violent lateral oscillations caused by improperly hooked-up controls. The next day he flew about 40 minutes. Source: Archangel narrative, p. 16.
  - **26 April 1962**: given as "First test flight" in the *same* monograph's "Timeline of OXCART Milestones", p. 48. The CIA's earlier history, McIninch's "The Oxcart Story" (https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Oxcart-Story.pdf), gives 26 April 1962 as the "unofficial, unannounced, maiden flight lasting some 40 minutes" and does not mention a 25 April hop. Roadrunners Internationale also uses 26 April. So 26 April is the first flight of real duration, and 25 April is the short hop that only Robarge records. airframes.json uses 26 April.
  - **24 April 1962**: the Smithsonian NASM SR-71 object page says Schalk "flew the single-seat A-12 on April 24, 1962, after he became airborne accidentally during high-speed taxi trials" (https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/lockheed-sr-71-blackbird/nasm_A19920072000). The CIA account describes it as an unofficial, unannounced flight made "in keeping with a Lockheed tradition", so do not repeat "accidental".
  - **30 April 1962**: the **official first flight**. Schalk flew 59 minutes, reached 30,000 ft and just under 400 mph, watched by CIA and Air Force officials. Sources: Archangel, p. 17; Lockheed Martin, "Creating the Blackbird" (https://www.lockheedmartin.com/en-us/news/features/history/blackbird.html).
  - First supersonic flight: 4 May 1962, Mach 1.1 at 40,000 ft (Archangel, p. 17).
- **Safe phrasing:** "Article 121, the first A-12 (60-6924), made its official first flight on 30 April 1962 with Lou Schalk at the controls, after an unannounced hop a few days earlier."
- **Lifetime:** 332 flights, 418.2 hours; on display at the Blackbird Airpark, Palmdale, California (Archangel, p. 50).

## 3. Myths and disputed claims to avoid

1. **"LBJ misread RS-71 as SR-71."** Disputed, and repeated even by the CIA. The official transcript of the 24 Jul 1964 news conference has Johnson saying "SR-71" (and separately "RS-70"): https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/the-presidents-news-conference-1053. A 2014 Beale AFB history article says Gen. Curtis LeMay preferred "SR" and the speech was changed beforehand, while press handouts still said "RS-71", which started the story (https://www.beale.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/667175/this-week-in-beale-history-sr-71-revealed/). But the CIA's own Archangel monograph (p. 53) says Johnson "mistakenly transposed the designator letters" and the Air Force "let the error stand". Safe phrasing: "Johnson announced it as the SR-71; the old story that he garbled 'RS-71' is contradicted by the transcript."
2. **"The SR-71 holds the absolute altitude record."** It does not. The FAI absolute altitude record for a jet that takes off under its own power is Alexandr Fedotov's 37,650 m (123,523 ft) zoom climb in the E-266M (MiG-25), 31 Aug 1977. The SR-71's 85,069 ft is for *sustained horizontal flight*. This Day in Aviation reports the FAI status of that category as "retired by changes of the sporting code". Smithsonian Air & Space: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/who-holds-the-altitude-record-for-an-airplane-141522931/. Safe phrasing: "highest sustained horizontal flight by an air-breathing jet".
3. **"Fastest aircraft ever."** Rocket aircraft such as the X-15 were faster. Safe phrasing: "fastest air-breathing crewed jet" (Smithsonian: "fastest aircraft propelled by air-breathing engines").
4. **"4,000 missiles were fired at it."** Airman magazine (2017) uses 4,000. Air & Space Forces Magazine and Beale AFB/DVIDS (2020) say "more than 1,000". Neither publishes its count. Use "more than 1,000" or present both.
5. **"No one ever died flying a Blackbird."** False. Lockheed RSO Jim Zwayer was killed when 61-7952 broke up on 25 Jan 1966 (Bill Weaver survived). CIA pilots Walt Ray (5 Jan 1967) and Jack Weeks (4 Jun 1968 US date, 5 Jun on Okinawa) died in A-12s, and Lockheed LCO Ray Torick drowned after the M-21/D-21 collision on 30 Jul 1966. The narrower Airman claim, that no *Air Force* SR-71 crew member was lost, is a different statement. See airframes.json for sources.
6. **"It had to refuel straight after takeoff because it leaked so much fuel."** The tanks did leak on the ground (Merlin: 10,000 linear feet of sealant that "leaked a considerable amount of fuel"), but SR-71 pilot Col. Richard Graham says the leak was not the reason. He gives tank inerting as the reason: full tanks push out air so nitrogen can pressurize the space as fuel burns. Other accounts cite tire, brake and engine-out margins (Roadrunners "SR-71 Fun Facts": "Most take-offs at 40,000 lb fuel load"). Sources: https://www.thesr71blackbird.com/Aircraft/JP-7-Fuel/why-did-the-sr-71-have-to-refuel-right-after-take-off; https://www.roadrunnersinternationale.com/carpenter/sr-71_mst_funfactsjun10.pdf. Avoid giving a single cause.
7. **"An aluminum airframe with titanium skin."** The Smithsonian SR-71 page says the titanium skin "shielded the internal aluminum airframe". This conflicts with NASA's historian (93 percent of structural weight titanium; heat "precluded the use of aluminum as a basic structural material") and with the CIA. Do not repeat it.
8. **"The A-12's first flight was an accident during taxi tests (24 April 1962)."** See section 1. Both CIA histories describe an unofficial, unannounced flight made by Lockheed custom: a hop on 25 April (Robarge) and a 40-minute flight on 26 April (Robarge and McIninch). The official first flight was 30 April.
9. **"The titanium came from the Soviet Union."** Partly true. The CIA says "some" of the base metal "had to be obtained covertly" from the USSR (Archangel, p. 11). Do not say "all".
10. **"The Blackbirds overflew the Soviet Union."** The CIA says the A-12 "was never used for its intended purpose of overflying the Soviet Union" (Archangel, p. 41). The Smithsonian notes that "when the SR-71 became operational, orbiting reconnaissance satellites had already replaced manned aircraft to gather intelligence from sites deep within Soviet territory". Do not claim Soviet overflights for any Blackbird.
11. **Serial format "64-17950".** This is genuinely disputed, not just a myth. The Smithsonian archives say the 64- form is erroneous and the correct form is 61-79xx (NASM.1990.0076). Joe Baugher's own lists contradict each other: the 1961 page calls 64- erroneous, the 1964 page says "It now appears that FY is indeed 1964". USAF and DVIDS captions often use 64-179xx. This pack uses 61-79xx, as NASA, CIA, Smithsonian and most museum labels do.
12. **"YF-12C" as a separate aircraft.** It was SR-71A 61-7951, loaned to NASA and painted with the fictitious serial 60-6937 (which really belongs to A-12 Article 131) to hide that NASA had an SR-71. Sources: NASA FS-030; Baugher 1961 list.
13. **"A-11" as a real aircraft.** On 29 Feb 1964 Johnson deliberately called the aircraft the "A-11", by agreement with Kelly Johnson, to protect the A-12. The aircraft shown were YF-12As (Merlin, AIAA 2009-1522).
14. **Small date conflicts to keep in footnotes.**
    - SR-71 first flight: 22 Dec 1964 (NASA FS-030, Smithsonian, Lockheed Martin, CIA timeline) vs "Dec. 21, 1964" (Air & Space Forces Magazine). Use 22 Dec.
    - USAF retirement ceremony: 26 Jan 1990 (DVIDS/Beale) vs 25 Jan 1990 (Air & Space Forces Magazine).
    - Jack Weeks' loss: 4 June (Archangel timeline) vs 5 June 1968. The difference is probably time zone.
    - LA to DC average speed: 2,124 mph (Smithsonian) vs 2,144.83 mph (sr-71.org records, matching Merlin's 2,144). The 2,124.5 figure is the coast-to-coast leg average in sr-71.org's list, so the Smithsonian may have attached the wrong average to the LA to DC time.
    - LBJ's "A-11" disclosure: 29 Feb 1964 (transcript, NASA, CIA Archangel) vs 24 Feb 1964 (McIninch). Use 29 Feb.
    - M-21/D-21 collision: 30 Jul 1966 (CIA) vs 31 Jul (Merlin). Use 30 Jul.
    - YF-12A sustained altitude record, 1 May 1965: 80,257.65 ft (NMUSAF, CIA, NASA). The FAI file gives 24,463 m (about 80,259 ft). The 80,257.86 ft figure sometimes seen was not found in any source.
    - Last A-12 operational mission: 8 May (CIA narrative) vs 6 May 1968 (CIA timeline).
    - D-21 programme cancellation: 15 Jul 1971 (CIA) vs 23 Jul 1971 (Wikipedia).
    - Number of SR-71 pilots: "only 86 pilots" flew operational missions (DVIDS/Beale) vs "only 135 pilots ever qualified" (FAI press release). These may be counting different things.
15. **Altitude record airframe.** Three airframes are credited with the 28 Jul 1976 sustained altitude record. 61-7962: IWM Duxford, seen in search-result text because iwm.org.uk blocks fetches (https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/cold-war/intelligence/sr-71-blackbird). 61-7963: Lockheed Martin's Code One magazine (2011) and habu.org, which say Helt and Elliott switched aircraft after a problem with their assigned one. 61-7958: This Day in Aviation (https://www.thisdayinaviation.com/28-july-1976/), which credits 958 with all three July 1976 records. Unresolved. Name the crew, not the airframe, in hero copy. The speed records in 61-7958 are not disputed.
16. **Bad flight-hour figures.** A DVIDS photo caption (2020) gives 61-7963 "6,014 flight hours". The sr-71.org hours list puts 61-7963 at 1,604.4, and the highest total for any Blackbird in this pack is SR-71B 61-7956 at 3,967.5. Do not reuse the caption figure.

## 4. Open gaps (not verified in this pass)

- **Blocked sources.** nationalmuseum.af.mil, af.mil (Beale, Airman), fai.org, iwm.org.uk and nro.gov block automated fetches. Facts from them were taken from search-result text or verbatim copies elsewhere. A human check of the NMUSAF fact sheets (SR-71A 61-7976, YF-12A, D-21B) and the FAI record files (8879, 3496) is the best next step.
- **sr-71.org was down (503).** Per-airframe hours and records were read from Internet Archive snapshots.
- **First flights not found:** A-12s other than 60-6924, 60-6925 (month only), 60-6930 (year only), 60-6931 and 60-6933. SR-71s 7954, 7957, 7959, 7962, 7966, 7969, 7970, 7974, 7977, 7978 and 7979.
- **Total hours not found:** 60-6934, 60-6935, 61-7950, 7954, 7957, 7969, 7970, 7974, 7977 and 7978.
- **YF-12A details.** Article numbers 1001 to 1003 and 60-6936's hours come from secondary sources only. The last USAF YF-12A flight date and the AIM-47 test dates were not confirmed in a primary source.
- **D-21.** Only 4 of the 11 or so surviving D-21s were checked against museum pages. The SENIOR BOWL target (Lop Nur) and crash sites rest on secondary sources.
- **SR-71 milestones.** No date found for initial operational capability, the GLOWING HEAT code name, or NASA's last SR-71 *research* flight (as opposed to the 9 Oct 1999 airshow flight).
- **61-7980's 2026 status** is reported by The War Zone and Aviation Week, not confirmed by NASA.
